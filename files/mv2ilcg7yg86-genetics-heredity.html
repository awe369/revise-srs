Genetics & Heredity

Why a child looks like the parents, written in one chain of ideas. Understand the chain once and every question in the PDF (and the ones that are not in it) becomes a reasoning job, not a memory job.

The whole chapter in one picture. Heredity is the copying of instructions. The instructions are written in DNA, packed into chromosomes, kept in the nucleus, and passed from parent to child through the egg and sperm.

The big picture: one chain of ideas

Genome > chromosome > DNA > gene > codon > base. Almost every "what is X" question in this chapter is asking you to place something on this ladder.

Where do differences between children and parents come from?

  • Crossing over: chromosome pairs swap pieces in meiosis.
  • Independent assortment: chromosomes get shuffled into the gametes.
  • Random fertilisation: any sperm meets any egg.
  • Mutation: a change in the base sequence. It is the ultimate source of all new variation.

That list answers Q16 and Q11 of the question bank. Asexual reproduction also has variation, but it is small and hard to notice, so Mendel's ratios need sexual reproduction (Q5).

Apoptosis vs necrosis (Q13)

Apoptosis

Programmed cell death. Planned, tidy, removes old or unhealthy cells and shapes the body (e.g. the webbing between fingers).

Necrosis

Death by injury or infection. Messy, cell bursts, causes inflammation.

Hook: Apoptosis = A Plan. Necrosis = Neglect or injury. Ageing and degeneration are processes, not the name for programmed death.

Mendel: the logic of inheritance

Traits are controlled by paired "factors" (genes). You get one from each parent, so both contribute equally. Which one shows depends on dominance.

  • Gregor Johann Mendel, an Augustinian monk at Brünn (now Brno, Czech Republic), is the Father of Genetics.
  • He crossed garden pea, Pisum sativum, over several years and presented his results in 1865.
  • Nobody noticed. In 1900 De Vries, Correns and Tschermak independently rediscovered his laws.
  • Why pea? Short life, many seeds, easy to cross, self-pollinating, and clean either/or traits.

DNA and RNA: what the instructions are made of

A nucleotide is three parts: sugar + phosphate + base. Join many nucleotides and you get a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA).

Complementarity is the key. Because A always pairs with T and G with C, either strand can rebuild the other. That is why DNA is "uniquely suited" to store and copy information (Q15).

Purines (2 rings)

Adenine, Guanine. Found in both DNA and RNA.

Pyrimidines (1 ring)

Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil. C is in both, T only in DNA, U only in RNA.

Hooks: Purines = "PURe As Gold" (A, G). Pyrimidines = "CUT the PY" (C, U, T). "U R in RNA" = Uracil lives in RNA. Gold chain G≡C is heavier (3 bonds) than A=T (2 bonds).

Chargaff's rule: free marks

Amount of A = amount of T, and G = C. So if a DNA sample is 30% adenine, then T is 30%, which leaves 40% for G + C, so G = 20% and C = 20%.

The central dogma

Copying DNA into RNA is transcription. Copying RNA into DNA is reverse transcription, done by the enzyme reverse transcriptase (RT). Translation reads the RNA in groups of three letters and builds the protein.

The genetic code

The code is read in groups of three letters called codons. Four letters taken three at a time give 4³ = 64 codons, but only 20 amino acids are needed. So several codons share one amino acid.

Trap (Q35): "Females play the major role in deciding sex because they have two X" sounds sensible but is wrong. Having XX only means every egg is X. The deciding variable is the sperm.

Jumping genes (transposons)

A transposable element is a piece of DNA that can move from one place in the genome to another. Barbara McClintock found them in maize and won the Nobel Prize in Medicine, 1983.

Spotted seeds in maize come from jumping genes. Roughly half of human DNA is transposon-derived, but most of it is inactive. When one does move it can cause a mutation (diseases like haemophilia and cancer have been linked).
Hook: McClintock = Maize Corn. 1983. "Jumping" is her word for transposition.

Three habits that win GS Science questions

  • Name the family first. Is this a "who", a "what", a "how many" or a "which is NOT"? Each family has its own table above.
  • Kill the odd one. In Q28 three options are DNA bases and one is an amino acid. In Q4 one is a plant used in a different famous experiment. The odd one is usually the answer to "which is NOT".
  • Pair a fact with a picture. McClintock + spotted maize kernel. Watson-Crick + twisted ladder. Khorana + test tube. One image per scientist.
